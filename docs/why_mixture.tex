\section{The old model}\label{part:old}

\subsection{What is modelled}

One shopping trip is fixed throughout. $\mathcal J$ is the set of products offered on that trip, and each
product $j\in\mathcal J$ belongs to one category $c(j)$.

A \emph{basket} is the set $S\subseteq\mathcal J$ of products bought on the trip. For a basket we write
$n(S)=|S|$ for its size, $n_c(S)$ for the number of its products in category $c$, and $m(S)$ for the number
of categories with $n_c(S)>0$. The allowed baskets form the set
\[
\mathcal S=\{S\subseteq\mathcal J:\ 1\le n(S)\le 120,\ n_c(S)\le 40\text{ for every }c\}.
\]
A model is a probability $p(S)$ for every $S\in\mathcal S$, with $\sum_{S\in\mathcal S}p(S)=1$.

\subsection{The parameters}

\begin{itemize}
\item $b_j\in\R$: the utility of product $j$ on this trip, built from its popularity, price, promotions,
  season, store and the household's tastes and history. On a given trip it is a fixed number.
\item $\phi_j\in\R^r$: the \emph{loading} of product $j$, a vector of dimension $r$ ($r=16$ on the real data).
  Products with similar loadings tend to be bought together.
\item $\psi_c\in\R^r$: a loading for each product of category $c$ beyond the first.
\item $\rho_c$, $f_c(k)$, $\rho_0(m)$: penalties on pairs within category $c$, on holding $k$ products of $c$, and
  on having $m$ categories in the basket. $\pos{a}=\max(a,0)$ and $\binom{k}{2}=k(k-1)/2$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The definition}

Each basket gets two numbers. The first is the sum of its product utilities and penalties,
\[
a(S)=\sum_{j\in S}\big(b_j-\tfrac12\lVert\phi_j\rVert^2\big)
-\sum_c\Big[\rho_c\tbinom{n_c(S)}{2}+f_c\big(n_c(S)\big)-\tfrac12\pos{n_c(S)-1}\lVert\psi_c\rVert^2\Big]-\rho_0\big(m(S)\big).
\]
The second is the basket's direction in loading space,
\[
s(S)=\sum_{j\in S}\phi_j-\sum_c\pos{n_c(S)-1}\,\psi_c\ \in\R^r .
\]

\begin{definition}[old model]
\begin{equation}
p_{\rm old}(S)=\frac{e^{F(S)}}{Z_{\rm old}},\qquad F(S)=a(S)+\tfrac12\lVert s(S)\rVert^2,\qquad
Z_{\rm old}=\sum_{S\in\mathcal S}e^{F(S)} .
\label{eq:old}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

$e^{F(S)}$ is the \emph{score} of basket $S$. $Z_{\rm old}$, the \emph{normalising constant}, is the sum of the
scores of all allowed baskets; dividing by it makes the probabilities add up to one. A basket's probability is
its share of the total score.

The term $\tfrac12\lVert s(S)\rVert^2$ is what makes products go together. Ignoring $\psi$, it equals
$\tfrac12\sum_{j\in S}\lVert\phi_j\rVert^2+\sum_{\{i,j\}\subseteq S}\phi_i\cdot\phi_j$: every pair of products in the
basket adds the dot product of their loadings, so products with similar loadings raise each other's
probability. The old model has no prior and no hidden variable: \eqref{eq:old} scores baskets directly.

\subsection{How the model is used}

\paragraph{Training} maximises the log-likelihood $\sum_t\log p_{\rm old}(S_t)$ over the observed baskets $S_t$ of
the training trips. For any parameter $\vartheta$,
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\vartheta\log p_{\rm old}(S)=\nabla_\vartheta F(S)-\E_{S'\sim p_{\rm old}}\big[\nabla_\vartheta F(S')\big],
\label{eq:grad}
\end{equation}
``observed minus what the model expects''. \emph{Scoring} a held-out basket needs $\log Z_{\rm old}$, and
\emph{generating} baskets needs draws from $p_{\rm old}$. All three need sums over the whole of $\mathcal S$, which is
far too large to list.

\paragraph{The taste variable.} The pair term couples every product with every other, so these sums cannot be
done directly. They become tractable after one identity.

\begin{lemma}[Gaussian identity]\label{lem:gauss}
Let $\N(z;0,I)=(2\pi)^{-r/2}e^{-\lVert z\rVert^2/2}$ be the standard Gaussian density on $\R^r$. For every $s\in\R^r$,
\[
e^{\frac12\lVert s\rVert^2}=\int_{\R^r}\N(z;0,I)\,e^{z\cdot s}\,dz .
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$-\tfrac12\lVert z\rVert^2+z\cdot s=-\tfrac12\lVert z-s\rVert^2+\tfrac12\lVert s\rVert^2$, and $\N(z;s,I)$ integrates to one.
\end{proof}

Apply it to the score: $e^{F(S)}=\int\N(z;0,I)\,e^{a(S)+z\cdot s(S)}\,dz$. Inside the integral the pair term has
gone; for a fixed vector $z$, called the \emph{taste}, the exponent $a(S)+z\cdot s(S)$ is a sum over products and
categories. This suggests looking at baskets one taste at a time.

\begin{definition}[basket law given a taste]
For $z\in\R^r$,
\begin{equation}
p(S\mid z)=\frac{e^{E_z(S)}}{Z(z)},\qquad E_z(S)=a(S)+z\cdot s(S),\qquad Z(z)=\sum_{S\in\mathcal S}e^{E_z(S)} .
\label{eq:cond}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
$Z(z)$ is the normalising constant of this law: the total score of all allowed baskets when the taste is $z$.
Writing out $E_z(S)$, each product contributes $b_j-\tfrac12\lVert\phi_j\rVert^2+z\cdot\phi_j$: the taste raises the
products whose loadings point its way. Because $E_z$ has no pair term, $Z(z)$ and draws from $p(S\mid z)$ are
computed \emph{exactly} by a dynamic program (category by category, tracking the basket size and the number of
categories).

\begin{proposition}[the old model through tastes]\label{prop:oldmix}
\begin{equation}
p_{\rm old}(S)=\int_{\R^r}\pi_{\rm old}(z)\,p(S\mid z)\,dz,\qquad
\pi_{\rm old}(z)=\frac{\N(z;0,I)\,Z(z)}{Z_{\rm old}} .
\label{eq:oldmix}
\end{equation}
$\pi_{\rm old}$ is a probability density on $\R^r$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$e^{F(S)}=\int\N(z;0,I)\,e^{E_z(S)}dz=\int\N(z;0,I)\,Z(z)\,p(S\mid z)\,dz$; divide by $Z_{\rm old}$. Summing over $S$,
$\int\pi_{\rm old}(z)\,dz=\sum_Sp_{\rm old}(S)=1$.
\end{proof}

So every computation with the old model is an average over tastes $z$ drawn from $\pi_{\rm old}$, with an exact
inner part for each $z$. In particular the expectation in \eqref{eq:grad} is
$\int\pi_{\rm old}(z)\,\E_{S'\sim p(\cdot\mid z)}[\cdots]\,dz$. In practice the average over $z$ was taken with Gibbs
chains that alternate $z\sim\N(s(S),I)$ and $S\sim p(\cdot\mid z)$, started from the observed baskets.

\section{Where exactly the problem is}\label{part:problem}

The problem is the taste density $\pi_{\rm old}(z)\propto\N(z;0,I)\,Z(z)$: the Gaussian density multiplied by the
total score $Z(z)$. This section shows that $Z(z)$ grows fastest for tastes that make large baskets attractive,
that this can give $\pi_{\rm old}$ several separate peaks, and that the procedures above see only one of them.

\subsection{$Z(z)$ grows fastest for large baskets}

\paragraph{Example 1.} Three products $A,B,C$, one taste dimension ($r=1$), loadings $\phi_A=\phi_B=\phi_C=1$,
utilities $b_A=1.5$, $b_B=0.5$, $b_C=-0.5$, no penalties. A product's contribution is $b_j-\tfrac12+z$, so at $z=0$ the
three contributions are $1,0,-1$ and at $z=2$ they are $3,2,1$. The scores $e^{E_z(S)}$:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccccc|c}\toprule
$S$ & $\{A\}$ & $\{B\}$ & $\{C\}$ & $\{A,B\}$ & $\{A,C\}$ & $\{B,C\}$ & $\{A,B,C\}$ & $Z(z)$\\\midrule
$z=0$ & 2.72 & 1.00 & 0.37 & 2.72 & 1.00 & 0.37 & 1.00 & 9.17\\
$z=2$ & 20.1 & 7.4 & 2.7 & 148.4 & 54.6 & 20.1 & 403.4 & 656.7\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Moving the taste from 0 to 2 multiplies the score of every basket of $n$ products by $e^{2n}$. $Z$ grows 72-fold,
mostly through the largest basket, whose probability $p(\{A,B,C\}\mid z)$ rises from $1.00/9.17=0.11$ to
$403.4/656.7=0.61$.

\paragraph{In general.} Write $\E[s\mid z]=\sum_Sp(S\mid z)\,s(S)$ and
$\Cov[s\mid z]=\sum_Sp(S\mid z)\,(s(S)-\E[s\mid z])(s(S)-\E[s\mid z])^\top$ for the mean and covariance of the basket's
direction under $p(\cdot\mid z)$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:grad}
$\nabla_z\log Z(z)=\E[s\mid z]$ and $\nabla_z^2\log Z(z)=\Cov[s\mid z]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$\log Z(z)=\log\sum_Se^{a(S)+z\cdot s(S)}$; differentiate once and twice.
\end{proof}
Moving $z$ in a direction raises $\log Z$ at the rate of the expected basket direction along it, and that rate
itself grows as $z$ moves on. With many products whose loadings point the same way, $\E[s\mid z]$ keeps growing
until the baskets hold all of them.

\subsection{$\pi_{\rm old}$ can have several peaks}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:old}
\[
\nabla_z\log\pi_{\rm old}(z)=-z+\E[s\mid z],\qquad \nabla_z^2\log\pi_{\rm old}(z)=-I+\Cov[s\mid z].
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\log\pi_{\rm old}(z)=-\tfrac12\lVert z\rVert^2+\log Z(z)+\text{const}$; apply Lemma~\ref{lem:grad}.
\end{proof}
Two forces set the slope: $-z$ from the Gaussian pulls the taste back towards zero, and $\E[s\mid z]$ from $Z(z)$
pushes it outward, harder the further out it is. Wherever $\Cov[s\mid z]$ exceeds $I$ the curvature is positive,
so $\log\pi_{\rm old}$ need not be concave and can have several local maxima (peaks). Every peak solves
$z=\E[s\mid z]$.

\paragraph{Example 2.} Twenty products in twenty categories, $r=1$, all with $b_j=-4$ and $\phi_j=1$, no penalties.
Each product contributes $-4.5+z$, the basket's direction is its size $n(S)$, and
\[
Z(z)=\big(1+e^{-4.5+z}\big)^{20}-1,
\]
the $-1$ removing the empty basket. The zeros of the slope $-z+\E[n\mid z]$, computed numerically:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{rrrl}\toprule
$z$ & $\E[n\mid z]$ & $\log\pi_{\rm old}(z)+\text{const}$ & \\\midrule
1.56 & 1.56 & $-0.63$ & peak 1: one or two products per basket\\
2.36 & 2.36 & $-0.68$ & valley\\
20.0 & 20.0 & $+110.0$ & peak 2: all 20 products\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Peak 2 is $e^{110.6}$ times higher. Integrating \eqref{eq:oldmix} numerically, tastes $z<5$ carry a fraction
$10^{-46}$ of the probability: under $p_{\rm old}$ essentially every basket holds all 20 products. The same is visible
in \eqref{eq:old} directly: the full basket has $a=20\times(-4.5)=-90$ and pair bonus $\tfrac12\cdot20^2=200$, so
$F=110$, against $F=-4$ for a single product. The pair bonus grows like $n^2$, the penalties only like $n$.

\subsection{Why training and scoring did not see it}

Suppose the real baskets hold one or two products. The Gibbs chains start at the observed baskets, so their
tastes start near peak 1. To reach peak 2 a chain must cross the valley, which is improbable at every step, and
the chains ran a few sweeps. So:
\begin{itemize}
\item the expectation in \eqref{eq:grad} is computed over peak 1 only; nothing in training sees, or lowers, the
  probability at peak 2;
\item the estimate of $Z_{\rm old}$, built from the same chains, leaves out peak 2, so $\log p_{\rm old}$ of held-out
  baskets is reported too high.
\end{itemize}
On the Dunnhumby refit peak 2 was the region of 120-product baskets: the reported held-out log-likelihood was
$-38.8$ per trip and $-53.8$ once the far peak was included.

The fault is therefore not in $p(S\mid z)$, which is exact, but in the taste density $\pi_{\rm old}\propto\N(z;0,I)\,Z(z)$:
a taste gains weight in proportion to the total score of the baskets it allows.

\section{The mixture model}\label{part:mix}

The mixture model keeps the exact basket law given a taste and changes only how tastes are weighted.

\subsection{What is modelled}

As before, the basket $S\in\mathcal S$ bought on one trip. In addition, every trip has an unobserved taste
$z\in\R^r$: what the shopper is in the mood for on that trip. The model describes how $z$ arises and how $S$
arises given $z$.

\subsection{The prior on the taste}

\begin{equation}
z\sim\N(0,I),\qquad\text{independently on every trip.}
\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
Most tastes are mild (near zero); strong ones are rare. Lasting preferences of a household are not in $z$ but in
the utilities $b_j$.

\subsection{The basket given the taste}

Given $z$, the basket follows the law \eqref{eq:cond} of Part~\ref{part:old}:
\[
p(S\mid z)=\frac{e^{E_z(S)}}{Z(z)},\qquad E_z(S)=a(S)+z\cdot s(S),
\]
where the normalising constant
\[
Z(z)=\sum_{S\in\mathcal S}e^{E_z(S)}
\]
is the total score of all allowed baskets under taste $z$, so that $\sum_Sp(S\mid z)=1$ for every $z$. $Z(z)$ and
draws from $p(\cdot\mid z)$ are computed exactly by the dynamic program.

\subsection{The probability of a basket}

Averaging the basket law over the prior:
\begin{equation}
p(S)=\int_{\R^r}\N(z;0,I)\,p(S\mid z)\,dz .
\label{eq:mix}
\end{equation}
It sums to one because each $p(\cdot\mid z)$ does. To generate a basket, draw $z$ from \eqref{eq:prior} and then $S$
from $p(\cdot\mid z)$; both steps are exact, with no Markov chain.

\paragraph{Example 2 under the mixture model.} The taste $z=20$ has prior density proportional to $e^{-200}$ and
is never drawn. Typical tastes lie in $[-2,2]$, where each product is bought with probability between $0.0015$
and $0.076$. The mean basket size is $\int\N(z;0,1)\,\E[n\mid z]\,dz=1.19$, and the full 20-product basket has
probability $2.3\times10^{-10}$.

\subsection{The posterior of the taste}

Scoring an observed basket $S$, and training on it, needs the tastes that could have produced it. By Bayes' rule
their density is
\begin{equation}
p(z\mid S)=\frac{\N(z;0,I)\,p(S\mid z)}{p(S)},\qquad
\log p(z\mid S)=-\tfrac12\lVert z\rVert^2+z\cdot s(S)-\log Z(z)+\text{const},
\label{eq:post}
\end{equation}
where the constant collects $a(S)$ and $\log p(S)$ and does not depend on $z$.

\begin{proposition}[one peak]\label{prop:mix}
\[
\nabla_z\log p(z\mid S)=-z+s(S)-\E[s\mid z],\qquad
\nabla_z^2\log p(z\mid S)=-I-\Cov[s\mid z]\preceq-I .
\]
Hence $\log p(z\mid S)$ is strictly concave and has exactly one maximum $\hat z$; moreover
$\log p(z\mid S)\le\log p(\hat z\mid S)-\tfrac12\lVert z-\hat z\rVert^2$ for every $z$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Differentiate \eqref{eq:post} using Lemma~\ref{lem:grad}. A covariance is positive semi-definite, so the Hessian is at
most $-I$ in every direction; a function with this property has exactly one maximum and the stated quadratic bound.
\end{proof}

Compare with Proposition~\ref{prop:old}. The term $\E[s\mid z]$ that pushed $\pi_{\rm old}$ outward enters here with a minus
sign, so it pulls in the same direction as the prior instead of against it. The observed basket contributes only
the fixed vector $s(S)$, which moves the peak but cannot create a second one. This holds for every basket, every
value of the parameters and every dimension $r$. In Example 2, for a basket of one product the slope is
$-z+1-\E[n\mid z]$; both terms decrease in $z$, and the single peak is at $\hat z=-0.10$.

\paragraph{Consequences.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Scoring:} $p(S)$ is estimated by importance sampling around $\hat z$, which gradient ascent always finds.
\item \emph{Training:} the gradient is a weighted average, over tastes drawn around $\hat z$, of ``observed minus
  expected given that taste''. The expectation given a taste is exact, so no region of baskets is left unvisited.
\item \emph{Generation} is exact, as above.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The two models side by side}

The old model implies the taste density $\pi_{\rm old}(z)=\N(z;0,I)\,Z(z)/Z_{\rm old}$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:oldmix}). It is not a
prior chosen in advance: it depends on all the parameters and on the trip, and it favours tastes with a large total
score. The mixture model uses the prior $\N(0,I)$ itself.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}\toprule
 & old model & mixture model\\\midrule
basket law given a taste & $p(S\mid z)$, exact & the same\\
weight of taste $z$ & $\N(z;0,I)\,Z(z)/Z_{\rm old}$ & $\N(z;0,I)$\\
density searched when scoring & $\pi_{\rm old}(z)$: several peaks possible & $p(z\mid S)$: exactly one peak\\
generating a basket & Markov chain & exact: $z$, then $S\mid z$\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{What changes, and what does not.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The two are different models with the same parameters; a fit of one is not a fit of the other.
\item In the old model a price change alters $Z(z)$ and so shifts which tastes are common. In the mixture model the
  distribution of tastes is fixed by the prior, and prices change only what each taste buys.
\item The mixture model removes the hidden peaks; it does not by itself make training correct. In the full real fit
  the held-out log-likelihood on all 1{,}024 validation trips fell from $-38.10$ at step 240 to $-38.76$ at the end
  (paired difference $-0.66\pm0.08$). That is a training problem, to be analysed separately.
\end{itemize}
